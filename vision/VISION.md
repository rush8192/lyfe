Vision for LYFE, the game of evolution and competition

# World

In LYFE, we create a living and evolving world. The world is a finite two-dimensional grid of tiles that represent slices of our world (see WORLD.md). The east-west `x` dimension wraps, while the north-south `y` dimension has bounded outer edges that preserve latitude and hemispheres. Cross-tile interactions are limited to edge-sharing neighbors and influenced by the compatibility of their attributes and current conditions. Each tile has different factors that influence the ability of organisms to live in that environment: temperature, moisture, terrestrial vs aquatic, volcanic activity, elevation, and more. Importantly, the world is resource-constrained rather than perfectly closed. Nutrients move between the environment and its organisms and are unavailable to competitors while held by a living organism, except through defined interactions such as predation. Death, waste, decomposition, and metabolic processes return or transform those nutrients. Geological and atmospheric processes may act as explicit sources or sinks for the portion of the world we simulate.

To start, each tile is a well-mixed environmental compartment: its conditions and nutrient pools are uniform throughout the tile. Individual organisms and dead remains still occupy specific coordinates within that tile. These positions allow direct interactions to depend on proximity and require an organism to reach the appropriate edge of its current tile before migrating to a neighboring tile.

Each tile also has a world coordinate and a signed elevation relative to sea level. Positive elevations are terrestrial; negative elevations are aquatic, with water depth derived from the magnitude of the negative elevation. The north-south component of the world coordinate acts as latitude. Fixed geography and long-term climate baselines combine with the time of year to produce current temperature, precipitation, surface moisture, and sunlight. Surface moisture is distinct from precipitation and can make a terrestrial tile seasonally habitable for moisture-dependent organisms.

# Organisms and Evolution

LYFE begins from a deliberately narrow set of primitive capabilities. V1 offers two founding metabolisms: sulfide-powered anoxygenic phototrophy and hydrogen acetogenesis. The sulfide path is a faster specialist in shallow, illuminated, sulfide-rich volcanic water, but it depends on that niche and has a more expensive route to broadly useful organic metabolism or oxygenic photosynthesis. The hydrogen path grows more slowly but functions without light and provides a cheaper route through organic uptake and fermentation toward a less volcanism-dependent existence. A few accompanying choices trade metabolic efficiency against environmental tolerance without erasing those identities. Founding organisms otherwise have a very limited set of tools: no locomotion or ability to sense the outside environment; no way to interact with other organisms directly; limited ability to store energy or withstand environmental changes; and only the simplest asexual reproduction.

Free sandbox begins with one player-chosen founding species by default. Survival begins with two independent founding species in a paired region of neighboring volcanic ocean tiles: the player chooses one metabolism, while an autonomous competitor receives the other under the same population, mutation-income, and simulation rules. Neither founder is descended from the other; both are grouped beneath a non-species abiogenesis origin in the tree-of-life presentation. Later speciation creates ordinary single-parent descendant branches.

A species in LYFE is defined by a single shared DNA; this shared DNA codifies all the gameplay-relevant physical attributes and behavior of every member of that species. Individual organisms have different positions, ages, health, energy, and other lifecycle state, but do not have heritable genetic differences in the initial simulation. Minor differences in appearance are cosmetic and do not represent different DNA.

As a species manages to survive and grow in number, the species will earn abstract mutation 'points', which can be spent on modifying the DNA of the species. Controlled and uncontrolled species earn points through the same rules. When a species mutates, random subsets of its populations in one or more selected tiles receive the new DNA and fork into one descendant species. Members of the old species are left behind unchanged, so the new species competes with its ancestor for resources. After the mutation price is paid, both species retain the full unused mutation-point balance.

A species' mutation-point generation is a function of its total living population, the average relative health of those organisms, and evolutionary modifiers in its DNA such as DNA exchange, sexual reproduction, and eukaryotic organization. An organism's relative health is grounded primarily in the amount of energy it has stored compared with its DNA-defined storage capacity, then adjusted by internal state and current environmental conditions. Energy capacity is deliberately limited but may increase through incremental storage traits or larger metabolic and cellular advances.

Some species are directed by a human controller, who chooses when to change a particular aspect of the species' DNA. Uncontrolled species make these choices autonomously; the more mutation points that have been accumulated, the more likely that a speciation event will occur. Their choices remain stochastic but are biased toward adaptations that address current environmental pressures, using recent death-risk profiles, realized triggers, and contributing stresses within the species as important signals. Autonomous evolution must distinguish unrealized risk from an actual cause and use only information available from the simulated history rather than knowledge of future outcomes.

Every new species retains a permanent link to its single ancestor, forming a tree of life that can be inspected even after branches become extinct. Species are reproductively isolated in the initial simulation and cannot breed or exchange DNA across species boundaries.

Evolutionary choices are irreversible. A species' DNA and completed speciation events cannot be edited or refunded. Sandbox switching moves control through existing branches of the tree of life rather than reversing evolution. A limited lineage-backtracking mechanic may use the same principle in a later version, but it is not part of v1.

DNA exchange and sexual reproduction are initially represented as abstract evolutionary capabilities rather than as exchanges or recombinations of individual genomes. They allow a species to accumulate greater mutation and speciation capacity, enabling broader changes when a new species is eventually created.

No progress comes without a cost. DNA is organized into distinct trait families, but those families may depend on or constrain one another. Traits can have cross-family prerequisites or incompatibilities, and a choice in one family may change the effectiveness or cost of capabilities in another. More complex organisms have ongoing metabolic or reproductive overhead and may depend on extracting additional resources from their environment to survive and thrive (see NUTRIENTS.md). A metabolism that functions efficiently within a certain set of environmental parameters may fail completely when the environment changes.

Organisms may die from senescence, depleted energy reserves, predation, or environmental conditions outside the tolerances encoded in their DNA. Conditions beyond a preferred range impose additional energy costs, while conditions beyond hard limits create an increasing chance of death each simulation turn. A death record retains the per-tick probability of every positive-probability cause actually evaluated before death and separately identifies every trigger that realized.

Reproduction transfers matter and stored energy from a parent into a new organism rather than creating resources. DNA determines whether reproduction resembles a true split, in which the resulting organisms divide the parent's reserves relatively evenly, or budding, in which the offspring begins with a smaller share. DNA may also change the minimum health required to reproduce, the base attempt frequency, and the additional energy cost of reproduction. Abstract sexual reproduction does not require mate proximity or individual-genome recombination in the initial simulation.

# Nutrients

To model the interdependence of organisms and the environment, we try to capture the key cycles that define the struggle of life on Earth for the major elements: carbon, nitrogen, hydrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and oxygen. Each macronutrient may be bound in an organic or inorganic form without requiring the simulation to track exact chemical compounds. Organic forms are generally easier for organisms to consume, while inorganic forms require specialized metabolic processes. We also track the presence and abundance of micronutrients required by more specialized forms of life, treating each micronutrient as a single bioavailable resource.

Energy is distinct from nutrients. Energy enters the living system from sources such as sunlight, geothermal activity, and usable chemical gradients, and ultimately dissipates. Organisms convert available energy into internally stored chemical energy, generally held in organic compounds that they can later consume through their metabolism. The nutrients contained in those compounds remain part of the world's resource cycles even as their stored energy is used.

Atmospheric gases are stored in tile-local reservoirs and move between neighboring tiles. Stable, abundant gases mix rapidly enough to become broadly distributed, while reactive or short-lived volcanic gases remain concentrated near their sources and may undergo rapid transformation or removal through an explicit sink. Ocean water and terrestrial surface moisture are environmental conditions and effectively inexhaustible background resources at the scale of the simulation. Dead remains are persistent cohesive entities that may be consumed wholly or partially before eventually decomposing into organic tile resources.

# Player Experience

LYFE supports multiple ways to engage with the same evolving world. V1 ships free sandbox and survival. Free sandbox allows the player to move control between species and lock selected species against mutation; it is lost early only if all life becomes extinct. Survival binds a single player to the chosen founder and its descendants in a struggle against both the environment and an autonomous founder using the other initial metabolism; in v1, the run is lost immediately when the controlled species becomes extinct, while extinction of the competitor does not itself end the run. Limited lineage backtracking may be added later using the persistent tree of life. Competitive multiplayer is a future mode that extends survival to multiple players, each bound to a different lineage. V1 does not implement multiplayer gameplay, but its server boundary, state model, command model, and simulation clock must avoid assumptions that would prevent it later.

The player guides evolution but does not directly command organisms. The initial controls are inspection, species selection where the mode permits it, mutation and speciation decisions, sandbox species locks, simulation speed and pause, and the ability to follow a random member of the controlled species. At the fastest speed, a thriving species should present a meaningful mutation decision every few real-world minutes.

The world is discovered through life rather than exposed completely at the outset. The player's starting tile and its edge-sharing neighbors are initially visible. A tile occupied by the currently controlled species has a live view of organisms, current conditions, and exact resource stocks and flows. Edge-sharing tiles and tiles the controlled species previously inhabited retain only a reduced view: fixed or basic composition and last-known information remain available, but organisms, exact current resource quantities, and variable conditions such as current temperature are hidden. Discovery persists as player knowledge, while live detail expands and contracts with the controlled species' distribution. This rule applies in every game mode.

Every world has a configured final simulation date. Reaching it concludes the run and evaluates which life and player-controlled species survived. Survival is successfully completed when the controlled species remains alive at the deadline; sandbox records the state of the world's surviving life without requiring a conventional competitive victory. The deadline may be presented as an impending world-ending event, such as an asteroid, or through a more whimsical conclusion, such as visiting aliens recognizing the life that endured. The result is determined from the final simulated state before any narrative epilogue changes the world.

The founding of life is framed by a whimsical storybook introduction in which repeated lightning strikes over a primitive volcanic ocean symbolize the energy, chemistry, time, and chance involved in abiogenesis. The introduction should acknowledge that the actual origin of life remains uncertain and should not suggest that one literal strike created life. The simulation starts after this narrative, with the established founding population or populations required by the selected mode.

# Biological Abstraction

LYFE is biology-inspired and internally mass-balanced. We simplify exact chemistry, historical sequences, and timescales in service of a comprehensible game, while preserving the core pressures of resource scarcity, energy flow, environmental stress, reproduction, competition, and evolutionary tradeoffs. Within the simulation's abstractions, nutrient matter remains accounted for unless it enters or leaves through an explicit source or sink.

# Simulation

We want to simulate individual organisms within the world and support a large number efficiently. Tens of thousands of concurrent organisms are the initial target; millions remain a stretch goal. We should make v1 decisions that preserve the long-term direction without implementing distributed or otherwise unneeded complexity before it is justified.

Each organism retains age and lifecycle state, concrete internal resource accounts, tile and within-tile position, velocity, current behavior, and any small capability state that persists across ticks. Health and current environmental stress are derived from this state, species DNA, and the surroundings. On every tick the organism updates its internal state, attempts recurring processes such as metabolism, chooses any conditionally available actions, and moves according to its velocity. Most biological outcomes are probabilistic and depend on DNA, organism state, local conditions, and nearby targets, while all resource transfers remain internally mass-balanced.

The simulation uses a configurable fixed-duration time step, initially one simulated hour. Organisms may live and die many times within a season, while species-level population and resiliency determine survival across months and years. Players can pause or change the real-world processing speed, but the engine always evaluates every tick and does not jump forward or skip activity.

Each world has a controllable root seed that governs both world generation and future random events. Given the same seed, simulation version and configuration, initial setup, and gameplay decisions at the same ticks, we should strive to reproduce the same world history. Saves and checkpoints preserve the random-state progression needed for deterministic continuation and replay.

Free sandbox and survival are the v1 single-player modes. Future competitive mode will support multiple players guiding different species in a shared world.

The authoritative simulation runs on a centralized server, while thin clients send decisions and render server-provided state. This boundary is required in v1, even for a local single-player deployment, because it supports varied clients, shared multiplayer worlds, and scaling the world with stronger server hardware without coupling the engine to a presentation technology.

Displayed organism instances should correspond one-to-one with actual simulation entities whenever they are rendered. Zoomed-out aggregates may summarize large populations, but they must be visually distinct from simulated individuals rather than presented as invented representative organisms.

Because understanding the simulation is central to play, the engine and interface must preserve and expose the history of key resource levels and flows. At minimum, the player can inspect tile-level resource quantities over time and see important inflows, outflows, transformations, biological consumption, and biological release.
